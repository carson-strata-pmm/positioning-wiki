# Category Creation Playbook

A step-by-step guide for positioning a B2B software company as the creator and leader of a new product category. Derived from Strata engagements with Agency, Eudia, and TeamOhana.

---

## Situation

Use this playbook when the client's product:
- Sits in the gap between two existing categories (neither fully owns the problem)
- Solves a problem that's structural, not incremental (existing tools fail for architectural reasons, not feature reasons)
- Requires the buyer to shift their worldview, not just their vendor

Category creation is the highest-effort, highest-reward positioning stance. It avoids feature-by-feature comparison but demands more rhetorical work to educate the buyer.

## Prerequisites

Before starting, you need:

1. **A validated hero problem that indicts a system, not a product.** The hero problem must describe a failure in the market structure itself (Agency: human-led CX model is broken; Eudia: legal operating model can't handle modern pace; TeamOhana: headcount managed through disconnected tools). If the hero problem only says "you need better software," category creation won't hold.

2. **At least one clear bright line between old and new.** You need a conceptual fork that separates the old approach from the new one: "bolt-on AI vs. AI-native" (Agency), "artificial intelligence vs. augmented intelligence" (Eudia), "spreadsheets/platforms vs. purpose-built" (TeamOhana).

3. **A named enemy.** Category creation requires defining what you are *not* as clearly as what you are. The enemy can be a structural approach (not a specific competitor): the human-led model, the disconnected tool stack, the billable-hour architecture.

4. **A category name candidate.** Even if it changes, you need a working name to test: "Superintelligent Customer AI Agent," "Augmented Intelligence Platform," "Headcount Management Platform."

## Step-by-Step Sequence

### Step 1: Name the systemic problem that no existing category solves

The hero problem must live in the gap between existing categories. Use the [[playbooks/hero-problem-playbook]] to develop it, but validate that it passes the **category creation filter**:

- Does the problem exist *before* any category exists to solve it?
- Would the buyer recognize the problem even if they've never heard of your product?
- Is the problem caused by the architecture of the current market, not by any single vendor's weakness?

**Decision point:** If the problem can be solved by a better version of an existing product, this is head-to-head positioning, not category creation. Stop here and use a different approach.

> **Agency:** "The Customer Value Paradox" -- companies hire more people to improve CX, but more people create more handoffs, degrading CX. No existing category (CRM, support tools, success platforms) solves this because the human-led model itself is the problem.

> **TeamOhana:** "The Headcount Headache" -- 70% of OPEX managed through spreadsheets because FP&A tools aren't built for recruiting and HRIS isn't built for finance. The gap between categories is the problem.

### Step 2: Indict existing approaches as structurally incapable

Each existing approach must be shown to fail for *architectural* reasons, not feature reasons. Build a structured indictment:

| Existing Approach | Why It Fails | Type of Failure |
|-------------------|-------------|-----------------|
| People / manual processes | Creates handoffs, inconsistency, doesn't scale | Structural |
| Legacy software (category A) | Built for X, not Y; can't span the gap | Architectural |
| Legacy software (category B) | Built for Y, not X; same gap from the other side | Architectural |
| Point solutions / bolt-ons | Layered on broken workflows; doesn't address root cause | Incremental |

> **Eudia:** Old solutions -- people, legacy software, and outside counsel -- all come with trade-offs. People are expensive and scarce. Software is generic and rigid. Outside counsel is slow and unpredictable. None address the fundamental speed-to-risk tension.

> **TeamOhana:** Spreadsheets lack accuracy and control. FP&A platforms (Anaplan, Adaptive) don't serve recruiting workflows. HRIS platforms (Workday, BambooHR) don't meet finance data requirements. Point solutions (Headcount365, CandorIQ) lack enterprise maturity.

**Decision point:** If you can only indict one existing approach (not 2-3), the gap may not be wide enough for a new category. Consider category redefinition instead.

### Step 3: Draw a bright line between old and new

Create a conceptual fork that makes the distinction visceral:

| Client | Old World | New World | Fork Language |
|--------|-----------|-----------|---------------|
| Agency | Bolt-on AI (layered on human workflows) | AI-native (rebuilt from the ground up) | "Autopilot, not copilot" |
| Eudia | Artificial intelligence (off-the-shelf) | Augmented intelligence (bespoke, human-centric) | "Augmented, not artificial" |
| TeamOhana | Disconnected tools (spreadsheets + platforms) | Purpose-built headcount management | "One platform, not patchwork" |

The fork should:
- Be expressible in a short phrase ("X, not Y")
- Make the old approach sound structurally inferior, not just older
- Position the new category as inevitable, not novel

### Step 4: Name the new category

Apply naming principles:
- **State what it is, not what it does.** "Headcount Management Platform" names a category. "Platform that manages headcount" is a description.
- **Use words the buyer already knows.** "Augmented Intelligence" works because both words are familiar; the combination is new.
- **Avoid jargon or internal product names.** The category name must be adoptable by analysts, press, and competitors.
- **Test: could a competitor credibly claim this category?** If yes, the name is broad enough to be a real category (good). If no, it's just your brand name (bad).

**Decision point:** If you can't find a name that feels like a category rather than a product brand, you may be forcing category creation where category redefinition is more natural.

### Step 5: Layer multiple opening strategies

Category creation requires more rhetorical work than other stances. Layer 2-3 opening strategies:

| Layer | Strategy | Purpose |
|-------|----------|---------|
| 1 | **Name a paradox** or **Surface a hidden cost** | Destabilize the buyer's current mental model |
| 2 | **Indict the status quo** | Show that existing approaches fail structurally |
| 3 | **Invoke an inevitable future** | Make the new category feel inevitable, not optional |

> **Agency:** (1) Names the paradox (more people = worse CX), (2) indicts human-led CX as architecturally broken, (3) invokes the future ("the future isn't more people -- it's Kai").

> **TeamOhana** is the exception -- it uses a single opening (surface hidden cost: 70% of OPEX on spreadsheets) because the problem is universally felt and doesn't require worldview-shifting. Simpler products with obvious pain can simplify the opening.

### Step 6: Build the differentiation architecture

Structure differentiation on three axes:

1. **vs. status quo** (manual processes, spreadsheets, human teams)
2. **vs. adjacent category A** (the tool that owns one half of the gap)
3. **vs. adjacent category B** (the tool that owns the other half)

Optionally add a fourth: **vs. point solutions** (new competitors in the same space who lack maturity).

> **TeamOhana's three-axis differentiation:**
> 1. vs. spreadsheets: real-time accuracy, security, no manual work
> 2. vs. FP&A/HRIS platforms: cross-functional adoption, breadth and depth
> 3. vs. point solutions (Headcount365, CandorIQ): enterprise-grade, broader features

### Step 7: Validate with proof

Category creation claims are the hardest to prove because the category is new. Prioritize:
- **Financial ROI from early customers** (TeamOhana: $294K savings)
- **Before/after use cases** (Agency: onboarding, service, success scenarios)
- **Industry stats that validate the hero problem** (Eudia: 79% of legal teams saw matter volume increase)

**Common mistake:** Launching category creation messaging without any proof. Agency's framework has a proof point gap (no named customers or metrics) -- this limits the narrative's credibility. Push hard for at least one named customer metric before launching.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Forcing category creation when category redefinition works.** Not every product needs a new category. If the buyer already has a mental model for what you do, redefine the existing category rather than building a new one. BrightEdge and Unity Industry chose redefinition over creation -- and it was the right call.

2. **Naming the category after your product.** "Kai" is not a category; "Superintelligent Customer AI Agent" is. The category name must be adoptable by the market, not owned by you.

3. **Skipping the hero problem and jumping to the category.** The buyer won't care about a new category unless they first feel the problem. The hero problem creates the demand; the category names the supply.

4. **Only indicting one existing approach.** If you only compare against one alternative, it feels like head-to-head positioning, not category creation. You need to show that the *structure* of the current landscape fails, which requires multiple approaches failing for different reasons.

5. **Layering too many opening strategies.** Two or three is the sweet spot. Four or more makes the opening feel like a lecture instead of a revelation.

6. **No named enemy.** Every category creator needs to define what it is *not.* Without a named enemy, the new category floats without an anchor.
