# Problem Pillar Playbook

A step-by-step guide for decomposing a hero problem into three pain pillars that form the backbone of a messaging framework. Derived from cross-client analysis of all 10 Strata engagements.

---

## Situation

Use this playbook after the hero problem has been named (see [[playbooks/hero-problem-playbook]]). The hero problem is a compression -- a single label for a systemic challenge. Pain pillars are the expansion: three distinct sub-problems that break the hero problem into addressable dimensions. Every Strata messaging framework is built on this pillar architecture.

## Prerequisites

1. **A named hero problem** that passes at least two of the three quality tests (name, inevitability, pre-product).
2. **Discovery data** -- interview transcripts, customer feedback, or internal documentation that reveals the specific ways the hero problem manifests.
3. **Clarity on the target buyer** -- pillars should reflect how the buyer experiences the problem, not how the product is organized.

## Step-by-Step Sequence

### Step 1: Identify three non-overlapping dimensions

Take the hero problem and ask: **What are the 3 distinct dimensions of this problem?**

Use the five pillar archetypes as a starting menu:

| Archetype | Question It Answers | Frequency |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------|
| **Capacity** | "Can we do enough?" | 8 of 10 clients |
| **Quality** | "Is it good when we do it?" | 6 of 10 |
| **Outcome** | "Are we achieving what we should?" | 5 of 10 |
| **Visibility** | "Can we see what's happening?" | 3 of 10 |
| **Cost** | "What are we wasting?" | 3 of 10 |

Most pillar sets use 3 of these 5. The most common combination is **Capacity + Quality + Outcome**.

**Decision point:** If two of your candidate dimensions feel like the same type of failure, they overlap. Merge them and find a genuinely different third dimension.

> **TeamOhana:** Data (visibility) + Control (quality) + Time (cost) -- three completely distinct dimensions. No overlap.

> **General Legal (weak):** P2 ("spending too little on strategy") and P3 ("perceived as cost center") both describe not being strategic enough -- they overlap. This makes the set feel like two pillars, not three.

### Step 2: Sequence from upstream to downstream

Order the pillars as **Input → Process → Output**. This mirrors how the buyer experiences the problem:

1. **Pillar 1 (Input/Capacity):** The upstream constraint -- not enough resources, bad data, no access
2. **Pillar 2 (Process/Quality):** The workflow failure -- broken process, inconsistent execution
3. **Pillar 3 (Outcome/Value):** The downstream consequence -- unrealized value, wasted spend, lost opportunity

| Client | P1 (Input) | P2 (Process) | P3 (Outcome) |
|--------|-----------|--------------|---------------|
| Agency | Can't reach all customers | Bad experience when you do | Don't realize value |
| TeamOhana | Data is bad | Process is broken | Time is wasted |
| Unity Industry | Can't create | Can't collaborate | Can't see what's built |
| General Legal | Can't keep up | No time for strategy | Perceived as cost center |
| BrightEdge | No bandwidth | Can't make sense of data | Can't get org buy-in |
| Snappy | No strategy | Operations are manual | Gifts don't land |

**Why this sequence works:** It builds a logical argument. The inputs are broken → so the process is broken → so the outcomes are broken. Each pillar feels like a consequence of the one before it.

**Decision point:** If the sequence doesn't flow causally, the dimensions may be wrong. Go back to Step 1.

> **Avoca is the notable variant.** Revenue capture (input) → Quality (process) → Staffing (capacity). The third pillar loops back to an input problem because for owner-operators, staffing *is* the output problem.

### Step 3: Build the cause-consequence stack for each pillar

Each pillar replicates the hero problem's three-layer structure at a lower level:

```
Pain Pillar Name (2-4 words)
    |
    Pain Statement (one sentence describing the sub-problem)
    |
    Causes (2-3 structural reasons this exists)
    |
    Negative Consequences (2-3 measurable costs)
```

> **TeamOhana's "Untrusted data" pillar:**
> - **Pain statement:** Headcount data is fragmented, error-ridden, and stale
> - **Causes:** Scattered data (across tools), bad data (manual entry errors), outdated data (no real-time sync)
> - **Consequences:** No visibility into true headcount position, security risk from shared spreadsheets

**Quality check:** Each cause should be structural (not fixable by trying harder). Each consequence should be specific enough to be measurable, even if no one has measured it yet.

**Decision point:** If you can't write 2-3 distinct causes for a pillar, it may be too thin. Consider whether it's a real dimension or a consequence of another pillar. BrightEdge's P3 ("limited cross-functional buy-in") had only one cause and vague consequences -- it reads more like a consequence of P1 and P2 than an independent pillar.

### Step 4: Name each pillar (2-4 words)

Naming conventions:

| Style | Example | Best For |
|-------|---------|----------|
| **Noun phrase (2-3 words)** | "Untrusted data," "Staffing challenges" | Most clients. Clean, referenceable, fits in tables. **Recommended default.** |
| **First-person pain statement** | "I can't keep up with the pace of business" | Enterprise buyers where empathy matters. Harder to reference in shorthand. |
| **Sentence fragment** | "Creation bottlenecked by lack of specialists" | When the pillar needs context. Works in narrative-heavy frameworks. |
| **Single word + modifier** | "Constrained," "Being left behind" | Emotional, identity-level messaging. Very personal. |

**Naming rules:**
- Name the failure state, not the solution ("Wasted time" not "Need for automation")
- Be specific enough that no two pillars could be confused
- TeamOhana's "Untrusted data / Lack of control / Wasted time" is the gold standard: each is exactly 2 words, each names a different dimension

### Step 5: Build value pillars by inversion

For each pain pillar, write the solved state. Choose one inversion type and use it consistently across all three:

| Inversion Type | Pattern | Example |
|---------------|---------|---------|
| **Negation** | Directly negate the pain | "Untrusted data" → "Real-time visibility" |
| **Aspiration** | Name the aspirational state beyond the pain | "Staffing challenges" → "Scale your front office" |
| **Mechanism** | Name how the pain is solved | "Collaboration barriers" → "One place to collaborate in 3D" |

| Client | Pain → Value (Type) |
|--------|-------------------|
| TeamOhana | Untrusted data → Real-time visibility (Negation) |
| TeamOhana | Lack of control → Unified process (Negation) |
| TeamOhana | Wasted time → Automated workflows (Negation) |
| Avoca | Underfilled job boards → Book more jobs (Aspiration) |
| Avoca | Inconsistent CX → Exceptional experiences (Aspiration) |
| Avoca | Staffing challenges → Scale your front office (Aspiration) |
| Agency | Limited coverage → Infinite capacity to serve (Aspiration) |
| Agency | Terrible CX → Single point of contact (Mechanism) |
| Agency | Unrealized value → Complete context on every customer (Mechanism) |

**Decision point:** If you can't write a clean value pillar for a pain pillar, the pain pillar may be too vague or too complex. Go back to Step 4 and sharpen the pain statement.

**Best practice:** The strongest pillar sets (TeamOhana, Avoca) use a consistent inversion type across all three pillars. Mixed types (Agency) still work but feel less rhythmic.

### Step 6: Validate with the four quality tests

| Test | Question | Failure Signal |
|------|----------|---------------|
| **Overlap test** | Can you clearly explain how Pillar 2 differs from Pillar 1? | If you have to work hard to differentiate them, they overlap |
| **Completeness test** | Do three pillars fully account for the hero problem? | If something important is left over, you're missing a dimension |
| **Independence test** | Could you solve Pillar 1 without solving Pillar 2? | If solving one automatically solves another, they're not independent |
| **Mirror test** | Does each pain pillar invert cleanly to a value pillar? | If the inversion feels forced, the pain pillar is too vague |

**Decision point:** If any test fails, go back to Step 1. It's better to restart the decomposition than to force a weak pillar set through the framework.

## How the Hero Problem Constrains the Pillars

The hero problem archetype often predicts the pillar architecture:

| Hero Problem Archetype | Pillar Architecture | Example |
|-----------------------|--------------------| --------|
| **Paradox** | Pillars decompose the paradox into its constituent failures | Agency: why more people = worse CX → limited coverage, terrible experience, unrealized value |
| **Gap** | Pillars describe what's on each side of the gap plus the gap itself | Unity Industry: creation bottlenecked (side A), collaboration broken (gap), visibility missing (side B) |
| **Hidden Cost** | Pillars itemize where the cost accumulates | TeamOhana: bad data → broken process → wasted time |
| **Constraint** | Pillars show what's scarce, why it's scarce, and what the scarcity costs | General Legal: can't keep up, no time for strategy, perceived as cost center |
| **Identity Burden** | Pillars name the roles being forced onto the buyer | Jane: admin consuming time, tools don't fit, patients need better care, isolation |

## Common Mistakes

1. **Pillar 1 restates the hero problem.** Snappy's P1 ("ad hoc gifting") is the same as the hero problem name. Pillars must decompose, not restate.

2. **Two pillars that are really one.** General Legal's P2 and P3 both describe "not being strategic enough." When two pillars overlap, the framework feels like it has two pillars, not three, and the missing dimension weakens the argument.

3. **Pillars organized around product features instead of buyer pain.** The pillar architecture should reflect how the buyer experiences the problem, not how the product is organized. If your pillars map to product modules, you've built a feature list, not a problem decomposition.

4. **Four pillars instead of three.** Jane is the sole exception with four pillars. Three creates rhythm; four loses focus. If you have four, look for two that can be merged or one that's really a cross-cutting theme, not a distinct dimension.

5. **Value pillars that don't mirror pain pillars.** Eudia CLO's pain-to-value mapping crosses over (P1 maps to V3, not V1). This works because a messaging map explains the relationship, but straight 1:1 mirroring is simpler and stronger.

6. **Thin cause-consequence stacks.** Each pillar needs 2-3 causes and 2-3 consequences. If a pillar has one cause and a vague consequence, it's underdeveloped and may not survive scrutiny in a sales conversation.

## Quality Tiers from the Corpus

### Tier 1: Clean decomposition, clean mirroring, clean naming

| Client | Why It's Strong |
|--------|----------------|
| **TeamOhana** | Tightest pillar set. Each pillar is 2 words. Each names a different dimension. Each inverts cleanly. No overlap, no gaps. |
| **Avoca** | Three distinct operational dimensions (demand, quality, supply). Detailed cause-consequence stacks. Strongest use case library. |
| **Agency** | Clean customer journey decomposition (reach → experience → value). Each pillar escalates in severity. Bold value pillars. |

### Tier 2: Strong structure, minor weaknesses

| Client | What Holds It Back |
|--------|--------------------|
| **Eudia CLO** | Strong pillars but cross-mapped pain-to-value. Compensated by messaging map. |
| **Unity Industry** | Good decomposition but pillar names are sentence-length -- harder to reference. |
| **Unity Games** | "Develop, deploy, grow" is excellent as a value framework but P1/P2 overlap on the pain side. |

### Tier 3: Structural gaps

| Client | What's Missing |
|--------|---------------|
| **General Legal** | P2/P3 overlap. Thin stacks. Empty proof section. |
| **BrightEdge** | P3 underdeveloped (one cause, vague consequences). |
| **Jane** | Four pillars. P4 is emotionally powerful but structurally unusual. |
| **Snappy** | P1 restates hero problem. Dimensions not clearly distinct. |
