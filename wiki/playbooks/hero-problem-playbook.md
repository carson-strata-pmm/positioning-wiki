# Hero Problem Playbook

A step-by-step guide for discovering, naming, and framing the hero problem that anchors a B2B narrative. Derived from cross-client analysis of all 10 Strata engagements.

---

## Situation

Use this playbook at the start of any positioning engagement. The hero problem is the first and most important creative output -- it determines the opening strategy, the pillar architecture, the category stance, and the buyer's emotional response to the narrative. Every engagement starts here.

The goal: produce a 2-4 word name for a systemic problem that the buyer recognizes as *their* problem before they ever hear about the product.

## Prerequisites

Before starting, you need:

1. **Access to buyers.** At least 2-3 discovery interviews with people in the target buyer role (or detailed interview transcripts). The hero problem must be built from buyer language, not company language.

2. **Understanding of the competitive landscape.** You need to know what alternatives the buyer considers so you can validate that the problem is structural (can't be solved by switching vendors) rather than incidental.

3. **A working hypothesis about the ICP.** You don't need a final ICP, but you need to know which buyer role you're targeting so the problem resonates with the right person.

## Step-by-Step Sequence

### Step 1: Identify the tension

In discovery interviews, listen for the two things the buyer is forced to trade off. The tension is always between two things the buyer cares about that they can't have simultaneously.

**What to listen for:**
- "We want X, but every time we try, Y suffers"
- "I'm always choosing between..."
- "The more we invest in X, the less we see from Y"
- Moments of frustration where the buyer describes a forced trade-off

**Write it as:** X vs. Y

| Client | X | Y |
|--------|---|---|
| Eudia | Speed | Risk |
| Agency | Scale | Customer intimacy |
| Jane | Patient care | Running a business |
| Avoca | Growth ambition | Front-office capacity |
| TeamOhana | Headcount spending (70% OPEX) | Process maturity to manage it |
| General Legal | Legal need | Affordability of expertise |

**Decision point:** If you can't identify a clear trade-off after 2-3 interviews, you haven't found the hero problem yet. Go back to discovery. Don't force a problem name onto vague frustrations.

### Step 2: Validate the structural cause

Ask: **Why can't the buyer solve this by trying harder?**

The answer must point to architecture, not effort:
- "The tools aren't built for this" (TeamOhana: FP&A ≠ HRIS)
- "The business model creates this" (General Legal: billable hours concentrate expertise)
- "The market is structured this way" (BrightEdge: search is shifting to AI)
- "The operating model requires it" (Agency: human CX creates handoffs by design)
- "The workforce is structurally volatile" (Avoca: seasonal, high-churn CSRs)

**The "trying harder" test:** If the buyer could solve the problem by hiring one more person, being more disciplined, or using their existing tools better, the structural cause is too weak. The problem must require a different architecture, not more effort.

**Decision point:** If the structural cause is weak, consider whether the client's real positioning is head-to-head (better execution) rather than structural (different approach). Not every product solves a structural problem.

### Step 3: Quantify the hidden consequence

Find the number the buyer doesn't know. This is the "lean-in" moment -- the specific, preferably quantifiable cost that makes the buyer realize the problem is bigger than they thought.

**Discovery questions to surface consequences:**
- "What does it cost when [the tension] goes wrong?"
- "How many hours per week/month does your team spend on [the process]?"
- "What happens to the opportunities you don't get to?"
- "When was the last time this caused a real problem? What did it cost?"

| Client | Hidden Consequence | Type |
|--------|-------------------|------|
| Avoca | ~$46K per CSR churn event, 90% of leads gone after 20 seconds | Financial |
| TeamOhana | $294K average in unapproved headcount costs, 70% of OPEX on spreadsheets | Financial |
| Eudia | Matter volume up 79% with flat budgets | Operational |
| Agency | 80-90% of enterprise customers underserved | Revenue at risk |
| Jane | "Work eats into your nights," "you feel isolated" | Personal/emotional |

**The visibility test:** The best consequences are ones the buyer hasn't measured. Making the invisible visible creates the "lean in" moment. A specific number ($294K) is more powerful than a big number ("millions in waste").

### Step 4: Select the archetype

Use the buyer's situation to choose the primary archetype:

| If the buyer's situation is... | Use this archetype | Signature language | Model client |
|-------------------------------|-------------------|-------------------|--------------|
| Their solution is making things worse | **Paradox** | "paradox," "irony," "the more you..." | Agency, Eudia |
| Two things that should be connected are disconnected | **Gap** | "gap," "disconnect," "between" | Unity Industry, TeamOhana |
| They're tolerating a cost they've never measured | **Hidden Cost** | "leakage," "hidden," numbers that shock | Avoca, TeamOhana |
| Something valuable is artificially restricted | **Constraint** | "constraint," "scarcity," "reserved for" | General Legal, BrightEdge |
| Their identity is being split by competing demands | **Identity Burden** | "overwhelmed," "feel," "you're not just" | Jane |

**Decision point:** Consider layering two archetypes for depth. The strongest hero problems in the corpus combine two:
- Agency: Paradox + Hidden Cost
- TeamOhana: Gap + Hidden Cost
- Eudia: Paradox + Constraint

Single-archetype problems work but tend to score lower on the quality tests.

### Step 5: Name it

Apply these naming conventions:

1. **Use "The [Noun] [Tension Word]" formula.** 7 of 10 hero problems use a definite article. "The" implies the problem already exists and is waiting to be named.

2. **Use tension words, not product words.** Gap, paradox, ratio, dual, headache, constraint, ceiling -- physics words, economics words, body words that imply force.

3. **Aim for 2-4 words.** Short enough to say in conversation, specific enough to own.

4. **Use alliteration or rhythm.** "Headcount Headache," "Customer Value Paradox," "Reality Gap" -- sonic patterns make names stickier.

5. **Use second person ("your") only for owner-operators.** For enterprise buyers, use third person ("the") to frame the problem as an objective market condition.

**Generate 5-10 candidates**, then test each against the three quality tests.

### Step 6: Apply the three quality tests

| Test | Question | What "pass" looks like |
|------|----------|----------------------|
| **Name test** | Can you say it to a stranger in the buyer's role and have them lean in? | They ask "what do you mean?" with curiosity, not confusion |
| **Inevitability test** | Does it feel structural and worsening, not situational? | The problem gets worse over time without intervention |
| **Pre-product test** | Does it resonate before the buyer knows your product exists? | The problem makes sense independently of any solution |

**Tier 1** (passes all three): "The Customer Value Paradox," "The speed-to-risk ratio," "The Headcount Headache"
**Tier 2** (passes two): "The Reality Gap," "The Scarcity Constraint," "Your growth is hitting a ceiling"
**Tier 3** (passes one or zero): "The Success Gap," "The dual responsibility," "Discoverability in the AI era," "Ad hoc gifting"

**Decision point:** If your name only passes one test, iterate. Go back to Step 5 and generate new candidates. If you can't get past Tier 2 after multiple iterations, the structural cause (Step 2) may be too weak -- revisit the tension.

### Step 7: Validate in buyer language

The final name must use words the buyer would use, not words the vendor would use.

- If the buyer wouldn't say "paradox," don't use "paradox"
- If the buyer says "headache" about this problem, use "headache"
- Test the name with 1-2 buyers informally: "We've been calling this '[name]' -- does that resonate?"

**Compounding language check:** Add language that implies the problem worsens over time:
- "compounds until growth stalls" (Avoca)
- "the more people you invest... the more challenging it becomes" (Agency)
- "matter volumes rise, budgets stay flat" (Eudia)

Compounding creates urgency without FUD.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Naming the problem after the product category.** "You need a headcount management platform" is a solution pitch, not a hero problem. "The Headcount Headache" is a problem. The problem must resonate *before* the product is introduced.

2. **Starting with the solution and working backward.** The hero problem must emerge from buyer discovery, not from the product team's feature list. If the problem only makes sense after hearing the solution, it's too product-centric.

3. **Choosing a situational problem over a structural one.** "Our competitors are growing faster" is situational. "The operating model creates handoffs by design" is structural. Structural problems justify new architectures; situational problems justify incremental improvements.

4. **Using vendor language instead of buyer language.** If the buyer says "it's a mess" and you write "suboptimal cross-functional data governance," you've lost the voice. Preserve the buyer's words.

5. **Settling for Tier 3 too early.** The difference between a Tier 1 and Tier 3 hero problem is the difference between a narrative that sells itself and one that needs constant explanation. The naming step is worth iterating on.

6. **Forgetting the consequence.** A named tension without a quantified consequence is an observation, not a problem. The consequence creates urgency.

## Examples from the Corpus

### Tier 1 Models

| Client | Name | Archetype | Why It Works |
|--------|------|-----------|-------------|
| Agency | "The Customer Value Paradox" | Paradox + Hidden Cost | Self-contained: the name *is* the argument. You don't need context. |
| Eudia | "The speed-to-risk ratio" | Paradox + Constraint | Mathematically precise. "Ratio" implies it can be measured -- natural bridge to the product. |
| TeamOhana | "The Headcount Headache" | Gap + Hidden Cost | Alliterative, visceral, universally felt. Easiest to say aloud and repeat. |

### Applied Pre-Engagement (Numeric)

Numeric's discovery brief applied this playbook before the engagement began:
- **Candidates:** "The Accounting Gap," "The Close Ceiling," "The Finance Blind Spot"
- **Structural analog:** TeamOhana (gap between categories, hidden cost)
- **Status:** Awaiting discovery validation
