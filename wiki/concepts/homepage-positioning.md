# Homepage Positioning

## Definition

Homepage positioning is the strategic work of making a B2B software homepage instantly communicate three things: what problem you solve, what you do about it, and why you're different. It is the highest-leverage single page in a company's marketing because it's where positioning claims are tested in seconds by real buyers. Strong homepage positioning turns a confused "what is this?" into a clear "this is for me."

## How It Works

Homepage positioning is not copywriting — it's strategic framing compressed into a single scroll. The homepage must make a series of moves in sequence:

1. **Name the problem or shift** — The hero section must establish why the buyer should care before introducing the product. This is the homepage's "opening strategy."
2. **Anchor to a category** — The visitor needs a mental model for what kind of product this is. Without a category anchor, use cases float without context.
3. **State what the product does** — In plain, concrete language. Not benefits, not vision — what it actually does.
4. **Differentiate explicitly** — Name the specific ways this product is different from alternatives. Vague claims ("better," "faster," "smarter") don't count.
5. **Prove it** — Social proof, metrics, logos, or customer quotes that make the claims credible.
6. **Give the buyer a next step** — A clear CTA matched to the buyer's intent stage.

## The Most Common Before Mistakes

Across the Fletch before/after corpus (7 B2B homepage repositioning examples), weak homepages consistently exhibit these failures:

### 1. No anchoring element for use cases
The homepage lists features or use cases but gives the visitor no category or problem frame to organize them. The buyer thinks: "I can see what it does, but I don't know what it's *for*."

> **CommandBar before:** "Covers a variety of use cases without an anchoring element" — the product could do many things, but the homepage didn't answer *what kind of tool is this?*

### 2. No clear problem owner
The problem described is real but isn't owned by any specific buyer role. Nobody reads the homepage and thinks "that's my problem."

> **LoneScale before:** "Messaging addressed a problem with no clear owner" — the pain was real but diffuse, so no single buyer felt targeted.

### 3. High-level benefits instead of concrete use cases
The homepage speaks in abstractions ("unlock growth," "drive efficiency") rather than naming specific, recognizable workflows or pain points.

> **LoneScale before:** "Focused on high-level benefits, not concrete use cases" — the messaging sounded impressive but didn't connect to anything the buyer was actively trying to solve.

### 4. Differentiation is absent or vague
The homepage describes the product without ever explaining why it's different from alternatives. The buyer assumes it's interchangeable with competitors.

> **CommandBar before:** "Differentiation is not specified" — nothing on the page told the visitor why CommandBar vs. any other tool in the space.

### 5. No strong point of view
The homepage is descriptive but not opinionated. It reads like documentation, not a positioning statement. There's no stance on what's broken in the market or why existing approaches fail.

> **CommandBar before:** "Lacks a strong POV" — the page described what the product could do but took no position on the market.

### 6. Unclear what the product actually does
The homepage is so focused on outcomes or vision that a visitor can't determine what the product literally does. This is especially common in AI and platform companies.

> **LoneScale before:** "Unclear who the product was for or what it actually did."

### 7. Problem-solution mismatch in page depth
Some weak homepages are either too thin (no substance below the fold) or too long (burying the value prop under feature sprawl). The page length doesn't match what the buyer needs to make a decision.

> **Concierge AI before:** Homepage was minimal (550px) — essentially just a hero with no supporting structure. The after version expanded to 3076px with full positioning architecture.

## The Strategic Moves That Fix Them

### Move 1: Category Anchoring
Assign the product to a recognizable category (or explicitly create one). This gives every subsequent claim a frame.

> **CommandBar after:** Anchored use cases to the product category "digital adoption tools" — immediately giving visitors a mental model.

### Move 2: Problem Ownership
Rewrite the hero problem so a specific buyer role recognizes it as *their* problem. Name the role, name the workflow, name the consequence of inaction.

> **LoneScale after:** "Clear focus on a use case someone owns and is actively trying to solve."

### Move 3: Concrete Explanation
Replace abstract benefits with a plain-language explanation of what the product does. Show the mechanism, not just the outcome.

> **LoneScale after:** "Simple, direct explanation of what the product does and how it works."

### Move 4: Explicit Differentiation
Name the specific dimensions on which this product is different. Use comparison language ("unlike X, we Y") or differentiation pillars.

> **CommandBar after:** "Differentiation is targeted and explicit."

### Move 5: Opinionated Stance
Take a position on the market. Say what's broken. Say what others get wrong. Give the buyer a reason to believe this company sees the world differently.

> **CommandBar after:** "Takes a highly opinionated stance."

### Move 6: Buyer-Resonance Filtering
Build the page around the capabilities that actual buyers respond to, not the features the team is proudest of.

> **LoneScale after:** "Messaging built around the part of the product that resonates most with real buyers."

## Homepage Positioning Rubric

Score any B2B homepage on these 7 dimensions (1 = weak, 3 = strong):

| Dimension | 1 (Weak) | 2 (Adequate) | 3 (Strong) |
|-----------|----------|--------------|------------|
| **Problem Clarity** | No named problem, or problem is generic/abstract | Problem exists but isn't sharp or buyer-specific | Specific, named problem that a clear buyer owns |
| **Category Anchor** | No category context; visitor can't place the product | Category implied but not stated | Explicit category with clear positioning within it |
| **Product Explanation** | Unclear what the product does | Understandable but abstract or benefit-heavy | Concrete, plain-language explanation of mechanism |
| **Differentiation** | None stated or only vague superlatives | Some differentiation but generic ("AI-powered") | Specific, targeted differentiation vs. alternatives |
| **Point of View** | Descriptive only; no market stance | Mild opinion but doesn't challenge status quo | Strong, opinionated stance on what's broken |
| **Proof & Credibility** | No social proof or metrics | Logos or generic testimonials | Specific metrics, named customers, concrete results |
| **Page Architecture** | Too thin or too sprawling; no clear flow | Has sections but sequence is unclear | Clear hero > problem > solution > proof > CTA flow |

**Scoring guide:**
- 18-21: Strong homepage positioning — ready for market
- 13-17: Functional but has gaps that reduce conversion
- 7-12: Needs repositioning work — visitors are bouncing or confused

## Examples from Client Work

The Fletch before/after corpus provides 7 examples across Sales Tech, MarTech, Product Management Tech, and AI categories:

- **CommandBar** — Repositioned from generic product page to opinionated digital adoption positioning. Key move: category anchoring + POV.
- **LoneScale** — Refocused from diffuse platform to specific use-case-driven page. Key move: problem ownership + concrete explanation.
- **UserGems** — Rebuilt to clearly describe problem, product, and differentiation. Key move: full positioning clarity (all three pillars).
- **Altura** — AI-Powered Bid and Tender Management repositioning in Sales Tech.
- **DinMo** — CDP repositioning in MarTech.
- **Concierge AI** — AI Answer Engine expanded from minimal hero to full positioning architecture.
- **Omnibound** — AI B2B Content Marketing Platform repositioned in MarTech (page actually shortened — cut sprawl, added focus).

## Patterns & Lessons

1. **The three-question test is universal.** Every weak homepage fails at least one of: What problem? What do you do? Why you? Every strong homepage answers all three above the fold.
2. **Category anchoring is the most underrated move.** Startups resist being "put in a box," but buyers need a box to evaluate you. The after versions consistently anchor to a category.
3. **Opinionated > descriptive.** The single biggest upgrade in the corpus is adding a point of view. Description tells the buyer *what*; opinion tells them *why it matters*.
4. **Buyer-centric filtering beats feature completeness.** Strong homepages show less of the product but more of what matters to the buyer. Features that don't map to buyer pain get cut.
5. **Page depth should match positioning complexity.** Some befores were too thin (Concierge AI), others too sprawling (Omnibound). The after versions right-size the page to the positioning architecture.
