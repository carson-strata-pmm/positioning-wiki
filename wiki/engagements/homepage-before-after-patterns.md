# Homepage Before/After Patterns

A cross-example analysis of 7 B2B homepage repositioning engagements from the Fletch before/after corpus, identifying the universal patterns that separate weak homepage positioning from strong.

| Company | Category | Sector | Primary Before Problem | Primary After Fix | Change Type |
|---------|----------|--------|----------------------|-------------------|-------------|
| **CommandBar** | Digital Adoption Tools | Product Management Tech | No category anchor, no differentiation, no POV | Anchored to category, explicit differentiation, opinionated stance | Category repositioning + POV addition |
| **LoneScale** | Sales Intelligence | Sales Tech | No problem owner, abstract benefits, unclear product | Owned use case, concrete explanation, buyer-resonant messaging | Problem ownership + concrete explanation |
| **UserGems** | AI for Outbound | Sales Tech / MarTech | Didn't clearly describe problem, product, or differentiation | Clear problem, product, and differentiation | Full positioning clarity |

| **Omnibound** | AI Content Marketing | MarTech | Long, sprawling page without focused positioning | Tighter, more focused positioning (shorter page) | Positioning focus + page compression |

## Pattern Taxonomy

Each before/after change maps to one or more of these change types:

### 1. Category Repositioning
**What it is:** Moving from "no category" or "wrong category" to a clear, defensible category position.

**Seen in:** CommandBar (anchored to "digital adoption tools"), DinMo (clarified CDP positioning), Omnibound (focused within content marketing)

**Pattern:** The before page either avoids naming a category (afraid of being boxed in) or uses a category that's too broad to be meaningful. The after page names the category explicitly and positions within it.

### 2. Problem Ownership
**What it is:** Reframing the hero problem so a specific buyer role sees it as theirs.

**Seen in:** LoneScale (from diffuse problem to owned use case), UserGems (from vague to buyer-specific)

**Pattern:** The before page describes a real problem that nobody in particular owns. The after page names the role, the workflow, and the consequence — so the right buyer immediately self-selects.

### 3. POV Addition
**What it is:** Adding an opinionated market stance where none existed.

**Seen in:** CommandBar (from descriptive to "highly opinionated stance"), UserGems (from unclear to differentiated)

**Pattern:** The before page reads like a product data sheet — it describes but doesn't argue. The after page takes a position on what's broken in the market and why existing approaches fail.

### 4. Concrete Explanation
**What it is:** Replacing abstract benefits with plain-language description of what the product does.

**Seen in:** LoneScale (from "high-level benefits" to "simple, direct explanation"), UserGems (from unclear to "clearly describes what they do")

**Pattern:** The before page uses outcome language ("drive growth," "boost efficiency") without explaining the mechanism. The after page shows the product's actual function in terms the buyer can visualize.

### 5. Page Architecture Restructuring
**What it is:** Fixing the depth, flow, or structure of the page to match the positioning needs.

**Seen in:** Concierge AI (expanded from ~550px to ~3076px), Omnibound (compressed from ~6401px to ~5436px)

**Pattern:** Page depth problems go both directions. Some before pages are too thin — just a hero with no supporting proof or explanation. Others are too long — feature sprawl that dilutes the positioning. The after versions right-size the page.

### 6. Buyer-Resonance Filtering
**What it is:** Cutting features/capabilities that don't resonate with actual buyers and amplifying those that do.

**Seen in:** LoneScale (messaging rebuilt around "the part of the product that resonates most with real buyers"), Omnibound (page shortened by cutting non-essential sections)

**Pattern:** The before page tries to show everything the product can do. The after page shows only what the target buyer cares about, based on actual buyer feedback or use-case specificity.

## The Universal Homepage Positioning Formula

Across all 7 examples, the after versions converge on the same structural formula:

```
1. OPENING: Name the problem or market shift (hero section)
   → Establishes "why should I care?"

2. ANCHOR: State the category or product type
   → Establishes "what kind of thing is this?"

3. EXPLAIN: Describe what the product does, concretely
   → Establishes "what does it actually do?"

4. DIFFERENTIATE: Name specific ways it's different
   → Establishes "why this one vs. alternatives?"

5. PROVE: Show evidence (logos, metrics, quotes)
   → Establishes "should I believe you?"

6. CONVERT: Clear CTA matched to buyer intent
   → Establishes "what do I do next?"
```

**The critical insight:** Weak homepages skip steps 1, 2, or 4. They jump straight to features (step 3) without establishing the problem (step 1), the category (step 2), or differentiation (step 4). The after versions never skip a step.

## Language Patterns: Weak vs. Strong

### What weak homepages say

| Pattern | Example Language | Problem |
|---------|-----------------|---------|
| **Outcome-first without context** | "Unlock growth," "Drive efficiency," "Transform your workflow" | No problem framed, no specificity |
| **Feature list as positioning** | "AI-powered. Real-time. Scalable." | Describes capabilities, not value |
| **Category avoidance** | "The all-in-one platform for..." | Refuses to name what it is |
| **Universal buyer** | "For modern teams" / "For enterprises" | No role specificity — nobody self-selects |
| **Vague differentiation** | "The smarter way to..." / "Built different" | Doesn't name what's actually different |
| **Passive voice** | "Workflows are streamlined" | No agency, no buyer in the sentence |

### What strong homepages say

| Pattern | Example Language | Why It Works |
|---------|-----------------|--------------|
| **Problem-first opening** | "Your [role] is spending X hours on [task] that should take minutes" | Names the pain, names the owner |
| **Category declaration** | "The [category] that [differentiator]" | Anchors understanding immediately |
| **Mechanism language** | "[Product] does [specific action] by [method]" | Buyer can visualize how it works |
| **Explicit differentiation** | "Unlike [alternative], we [specific difference]" | Directly addresses comparison shopping |
| **Buyer-specific address** | "For [role] at [company type] who [trigger event]" | Right buyer self-selects in 3 seconds |
| **Opinionated stance** | "[Common approach] is broken because [reason]" | Shows the company has a point of view |

## Cross-Pattern Observations

### What always gets cut
- Generic benefit statements that could apply to any product
- Feature sections that don't map to a buyer use case
- "How it works" sections that are really "all the things it can do" sections
- Multiple CTAs competing for attention
- Logos or proof points placed without narrative context

### What always gets added
- A problem statement or market-shift framing in the hero
- An explicit category label or positioning line
- Differentiation language (often as pillars or a comparison)
- Specificity — named roles, named workflows, named outcomes
- An opinionated stance on the status quo

### The before/after asymmetry
The before pages are almost always **too wide** (trying to address too many use cases, too many buyers, too many features). The after pages are almost always **narrower but deeper** (focused on one buyer, one problem, one category — but proving it thoroughly).

This is the fundamental trade-off: **breadth feels safer but converts worse.** Narrowing the positioning feels risky ("but we also do X!") but actually converts better because the right buyer sees themselves immediately.
